# Case study: a multi-character dance-sequence recreation

A 132.54-second, 24 fps, 4:3 dance excerpt was recreated with an approved lead and a large supporting cast. The final timeline had 3,181 frames across 54 original shot ranges, without original-RGB placeholders. The original acceptance record still marked all shots **accepted with limits**: completed coverage did not mean exact choreography or perfect extras.

## Direct replacement was not enough

Adding character sheets did not guarantee identity-to-body assignment. Trials misplaced the lead, retained recognizable original extras, altered the background and compressed motion timing. A second pass over an existing take sometimes retained its first-frame defect and introduced more timing differences. Increasing prompt length did not remove the need for review.

## Separated controls improved the workflow

Depth served as a motion/spatial reference, cleaned scene images supplied environment appearance, approved character images supplied identity, and composed opening anchors controlled initial blocking. A full-length depth film was explicitly aligned and cut to the requested interval. For short editorial shots, generated clips could be longer than the final owned frame range; surplus frames were excluded rather than stretching motion.

These inputs remained generative guidance. Cleaned plates reconstructed unseen regions; Depth did not guarantee exact feet, head direction or camera parallax. Some distant duplicates were accepted only after the user relaxed that requirement.

## Hard shots needed different repairs

An entrance shot repeatedly produced a late or oversized lead. A colored body control improved the trajectory but left a gray figure; a later repaint created two leads. A supported alternative video-editing model on the already-generated motion base finally produced one intended lead with better scene integration. This was a local, reviewed success, not evidence of general automatic tracking.

A 25-frame close-up later failed identity review at high resolution. The repair used a corrected opening anchor **and** the formal identity reference **and** matched Depth. The selected frames were checked before and after upscale. A small expression/phase difference remained disclosed. This demonstrated why “anime style” and “the approved character identity” need separate judgments.

## Finishing was independently verified

The first assembly used one continuous soundtrack, encoded once. Later visual revisions preserved the approved AAC packets. The 4:3 final used 2880×2160, preserving the ratio instead of forcing a 3840×2160 preset. An approved logo was added only after identity and timing fixes. Technical decode, frame/PTS coverage, audio packet equality, sampled visual inspection and end-of-file player checks were separate gates.

## Limits and release scope

Unexplained asset registration errors did not expose their root cause. The workflow does not infer copyright or format causes from a generic rejection, and does not teach representation changes as moderation bypasses. It also does not prove scientific depth accuracy, automated whole-body tracking, zero crowd duplication or pixel-exact motion.

This text and the self-contained skill folder omit source footage, music, character images, private task/asset identifiers, personal paths and account balances. The separate Gallery includes a complete silent preview with its existing watermark and an extracted poster; those files have their own media notice and are excluded from MIT. The case study reports an observed production method without licensing the underlying media rights.
